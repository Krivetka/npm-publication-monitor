Introduction

Relevance and problem. The npm registry is the supply chain of practically every JavaScript application. In the second quarter of 2026 it accounted for 96.6 % of all malicious open-source packages recorded by Sonatype [1]. The registry's answer, a content scan of every new package before it becomes installable (live since 28 July 2026 [2]), did not stop the ChainDrop worm a week later: 444 packages and 2 212 versions were published in under four hours from compromised but legitimate accounts, with valid provenance attestations [3], [4]. A content scanner cannot tell a stolen credential from its owner. What distinguishes such publications is their metadata — who published, when, in what rhythm, into which release lines — and none of the services examined in Section 1.3 watches this layer continuously for the consumer. Three groups have the problem: security engineers of organisations that consume npm packages learn about a compromised package from the news, when the evidence is already gone; maintainers have no signal that their own package was published by someone else; researchers cannot measure the residual risk after the registry's gate, because the registry deletes the malicious versions. The problem matters now because the attacks of 2026 passed a gate that did not exist a year earlier, so the residual risk after that gate is unmeasured.

Goal. To analyse how suspicious npm publications are detected today and to specify verifiable requirements for a continuous, metadata-based monitoring service that gives security engineers an actionable signal before the advisory. The bachelor project as a whole designs, implements and evaluates this service; this block covers its analysis and requirements.

Objectives:

  1. describe how suspicious publications are detected today and where time, signal and evidence are lost (Section 1.1);
  2. review the sources and six analogs and derive what a new solution must do (Sections 1.2–1.4);
  3. state the vision, the business goals with measurable KPIs, the stakeholders and the scope (Sections 2.1–2.3);
  4. specify the solution concept and the functional requirements as epics, user stories with acceptance criteria and use cases (Sections 2.4–2.6);
  5. specify the non-functional and legal requirements, the risks and the way the result will be verified (Sections 2.7–2.10).

Object and subject. The object is the process of detecting suspicious publications in the public npm registry: from the moment a version is published to the moment a security engineer has evidence to act on. The subject is the automatable side of that process — scoring the stream of publication events from metadata and package history, selecting a small set of candidates for enrichment, and producing explainable alerts with durable evidence.

Methods. Requirements were gathered by four methods. Document analysis: the project description, the registry's API documentation and announcements [2], [5], incident write-ups [3], [6] and the standards on provenance and secure development [7]–[9]. Measurement: a probe of the live change feed, package-document sizes and external-API quotas, run before the design (Section 1.2). Benchmarking: six analog tools examined and tried out (Section 1.3). Expert consultation: two sessions with the supervisor established the attack classes, the two-stage architecture and the placement of version rules; no external interviews were held (Section 2.8).

Structure. Chapter 1 analyses the current process, the sources and the analogs, and lists the problems to solve. Chapter 2 states the requirements: vision and KPIs, stakeholders, scope, the solution concept, functional and non-functional requirements, legal constraints, risks and verification. Appendix A holds the backlog, Appendix B the diagrams at full size.

Chapter 1. Analytical part

1.1 Current state of the problem

Who is involved. Three groups meet at the moment a package is published to the public npm registry. The registry (npm, Inc.) accepts the publication and, since 28 July 2026, scans the content of every new package before it becomes installable [2]. Consumers — application security engineers and developers — learn about dangerous packages from vulnerability databases, dependency scanners and the news. Maintainers own the packages and the accounts that publish them; when an account or a release pipeline is compromised, the maintainer is usually the last to know.

How the task is solved today (AS-IS). Figure 1.1 shows the process as it runs now, with four lanes: attacker, registry, security vendors and the security engineer of a consuming organisation. The attacker obtains a publishing path — a stolen token, a hijacked CI pipeline or a look-alike name — and publishes. The registry's content scan either blocks a package it can recognise, holds it for manual review, or lets it through. For the two attack classes studied in this project the scan lets the package through by construction: in a compromised-publisher attack the content is signed by the legitimate pipeline and carries a valid provenance attestation [7], [8]; in a worm the payload is delivered through a dependency chain the scanner judges file by file. Security vendors notice the campaign from telemetry or from a researcher's report, publish an advisory, and the registry removes the malicious versions. Only then does the consumer's tooling react: npm audit and similar scanners compare the lock file with the advisory database [10].

Where time and data are lost.

  • Time. Between the first malicious publication and the advisory lie hours; in the ChainDrop campaign of 4 August 2026 the first malicious version was published at 09:35 UTC and by 13:20 the worm had published malicious versions of 433 further packages [4]. During that window every npm install of an affected version succeeds and no consumer tool complains.
  • Signal. The metadata of a malicious publication is anomalous from the first second — a publisher with no history, three versions in non-adjacent release lines within a minute, a release after years of silence, a lifecycle script that appears out of nowhere [11], [12] — but no service watches this layer continuously for the whole registry. The registry's own change feed exposes it to anyone [5].
  • Evidence. The registry purges malicious versions. Their publication times survive only in the time field of the package document, while the manifests and the publisher of each version are gone. A later investigation cannot reconstruct what happened, and a researcher cannot build a dataset from the registry alone: the purged versions of node-ipc keep only their dates (Section 1.3).
  • Effort. Every consuming organisation repeats the same investigation of the same package after the same advisory, because the evidence was never captured once.

Figure 1.1 — AS-IS process of detecting a malicious npm publication (UML activity diagram; full size in Appendix B)

TO-BE in short. A monitoring service reads the registry change feed continuously. A first stage scores every event without network requests from its own journal: rhythm of publications, similarity of the name to popular packages, revision jumps, watchlist membership. Only the few percent of events that score as candidates are enriched from external sources under per-source quotas: publisher history, maintainers, manifest changes, release lines, repository state. An alert with a decomposed score and an evidence snapshot reaches the analyst minutes after the publication, before the advisory exists and before the registry purges the version. The analyst's verdict is recorded and becomes the ground truth for measuring the service. Figure 1.1 shows this TO-BE lane at the bottom of the same diagram.

1.2 Review of sources

The sources fall into five groups. For each group the review states what it contributes and where it stops; the requirements of Chapter 2 refer back to these conclusions.

Taxonomies and empirical studies. Ohm et al. reviewed 174 malicious packages from npm, PyPI and RubyGems and built the first attack tree of open-source supply-chain attacks; most packages trigger on installation, and typosquatting and account compromise are the dominant vectors [11]. Zimmermann et al. measured the npm dependency graph and showed that compromising one of a few hundred maintainer accounts reaches thousands of packages [13]. Duan et al. reported that metadata and install-time behaviour already separate a large share of malicious packages [14]. Ladisa et al. systematised 107 attack vectors and named publishing from a compromised account and manipulation of release lines among the vectors that content signing does not close [12]. Zahan et al. identified "weak links" in npm — inactive maintainers, expired domains, install scripts — as signals measurable from metadata [15]. Conclusion: the attack classes of this project are known, and the metadata signals that expose them are listed, but as static package properties, not as events in a continuous stream.

Detection approaches. Sejfia and Schäfer trained a classifier on package contents with practical precision on npm, at the price of a tarball and a labelled corpus [16]. Vu et al. (LastPyMile) detect the discrepancy between the source repository and the published artefact, a strong signal for a hijacked pipeline, at the price of cloning the repository for every release [17]. Taylor et al. (TypoGard) detect typosquatting from name distance and popularity at install time [18]. Conclusion: each approach covers one attack class, most need the content or the repository, none runs over the whole registry in real time, and none keeps evidence after the registry removes the version. Chapter 2 therefore requires a two-stage design, explainable features instead of a trained model, and evidence snapshots at observation time.

Industry reports and incident write-ups. Sonatype attributes 96.6 % of malicious open-source packages in Q2 2026 to npm [1]. The write-ups of ChainDrop [4], [19] (Aikido reported it as a new wave of the Shai-Hulud worm [3]) and node-ipc [6], [20] give the timings used for the replay scenarios of the evaluation. ChainDrop: 11 packages in the first wave, 433 more by 13:20, 444 packages and 2 212 versions in under four hours [4]; Aikido counted 1 381 versions in its first update [3]. node-ipc: three versions into non-adjacent lines on one day, from a maintainer account that had published none of the recent releases [20]. Three incidents of 2025 are kept apart as held-out scenarios, not used to design or calibrate the features: the chalk/debug maintainer compromise [21], the first Shai-Hulud worm [22] and the nx release-pipeline compromise [23]. The registry's announcement of pre-publication scanning [2] states its own limit: it blocks the malware it can recognise. Conclusion: the incidents of 2026 passed a content gate already in place; the residual risk after that gate is what the project measures.

Standards and provenance. SLSA provenance [8] and npm provenance statements [7] prove that an artefact was built by a given pipeline from a given commit — authenticity, not intent: ChainDrop's poisoned versions carried valid attestations because the pipeline itself was compromised. NIST SP 800-218 [9] frames the consumer's duty to verify third-party components but prescribes no mechanism for the hours before an advisory. Conclusion: provenance is recorded as evidence but has no weight in the risk score.

Data sources and their limits. The registry API documents the _changes feed and the package document [5]; deps.dev [24], ecosyste.ms [25], RDAP [26] and the GitHub REST API [27] expose dependency, repository, domain-registration and commit data under rate limits. Measurements made for this project in October 2026 set the design's numbers: the feed carries 1.0–1.4 events per second (90 000–120 000 events a day) with no payload beyond the package name and a revision number; a package document is 71 KB at the median and 8 MB at the 99th percentile, so one per event would cost 18–27 GB a day; GitHub allows 5 000 requests an hour with a token and 60 without. Conclusion: enriching every event is impossible within quotas, which determines the two-stage architecture and the budget layer of Chapter 2. Legal and ethical sources — the GDPR [28] and WCAG 2.1 [29] — are applied in Section 2.8.

1.3 Analysis of analogs

Six solutions were examined and all six were tried out, five of them on the same reference package, node-ipc, which had three malicious versions published and purged on 14 May 2026 [6]; the figures show what each tool reports five months later.

npm audit (open source, part of the npm client) compares the dependency tree of a project with the GitHub Advisory Database. In the trial project with lodash@4.17.15, minimist@1.2.5 and node-ipc@9.2.1 it reported two vulnerabilities, both known for years, and nothing about the node-ipc incident (Figure 1.2): no advisory covers the purged versions, so the tool has nothing to compare with. It works only after disclosure and only for packages already in a project.


Figure 1.2 — npm audit on the trial project: two known vulnerabilities, nothing about node-ipc
Figure 1.3 — GuardDog scan of node-ipc from the Docker image: content indicators only

GuardDog (Datadog, open source) [30] scans the content of a package tarball with semantic rules for malicious patterns. Run from its Docker image against node-ipc, it reported three low-risk indicators of "system information collection" in the legitimate code (hostname and user name used to build the socket path) and an overall assessment of 3.3/10 (Figure 1.3). The tool sees content, not history; it cannot know that the publishing account differed from the previous releases, and it cannot scan a version the registry has already removed.

Snyk Advisor / Snyk Vulnerability Database (commercial, free tier) [31] shows a package health score from security, popularity, maintenance and community signals. For node-ipc it shows 76/100, "no known security issues" in the latest version, and a banner that "this package was involved in a security incident resulting in compromised versions being published" (Figure 1.4). The banner is a manually curated note added after the incident; the compromised versions themselves are absent from the version list because the registry purged them.


Figure 1.4 — Snyk Advisor page of node-ipc: a curated incident note, no trace of the purged versions
Figure 1.5 — deps.dev page of node-ipc: no advisories, a repository scorecard

deps.dev / OpenSSF Scorecard (Google and the Open Source Security Foundation, open data) shows advisories, licences, dependencies and a Scorecard of repository practices. For node-ipc it reports "no advisories detected" and a Scorecard of 6.1/10 (Figure 1.5). The data describes the repository and the dependency graph, not individual publications.

Aikido Intel and Aikido Safe Chain (Aikido Security; the feed is public, Safe Chain is open source). Aikido Intel is a feed of malware and vulnerabilities that the vendor detects by scanning new packages, published within hours of the publication; its npm malware tab on 28 October 2026 listed packages such as identity-two-factor@999.0.0 and @zmtcdn/ui@9999.0.7 flagged 9–12 hours earlier (Figure 1.6) [32]. Safe Chain wraps the package manager and refuses to install versions that the feed lists; it also hides versions younger than a minimum age during resolution [33]. In the trial (Figure 1.7) Safe Chain installed node-ipc@9.2.1 and minimist@1.2.5 without a remark. For two versions listed as malware that morning, npm reported "no matching version" and Safe Chain noted that "some package versions were suppressed due to minimum package age". The registry had already removed them, and the age rule had hidden any remaining fresh version. Safe Chain is the only analog with a temporal metadata rule, applied at install time on the consumer's machine; it does not look at the publisher or the history of the package, and the feed behind it is still a list of recognised malware.


Figure 1.6 — Aikido Intel, npm malware tab: detections published hours after the malicious versions
Figure 1.7 — Aikido Safe Chain in a container: benign versions pass; listed versions are already gone and suppressed by the minimum-age rule

Socket.dev (commercial, free tier for open source) analyses package contents at install and pull-request time and flags behaviours such as install scripts, network access, obfuscation and, in its newer releases, publisher-related signals such as a new maintainer [34]. Its page for node-ipc (Figure 1.8, left) shows a Supply Chain Security score of 99 for the latest version and a banner "this package has malicious versions linked to the ongoing node-ipc supply chain attack" naming 9.1.6, 9.2.3 and 12.0.1 with a link to a campaign page. The Alerts tab (Figure 1.8, right) lists the behaviours its content analysis found in the latest, benign version: network access, debug access, environment-variable access, file-system access and URL strings, all marked "supply chain risk". Socket is the closest analog in intent: by its own account it reacted to the incident within minutes [20]. It did so as a content scanner on the consumer's side, and the attribution to a campaign was curated afterwards. The purged versions themselves are no longer analysable.

What is missing. All six analogs are either reactive (they need an advisory, a curated note or the vendor's own detection list) or content-based (they need the tarball, which the attacker controls and the registry later removes). The fastest of them, Aikido Intel, reports hours after the publication and gives the consumer a label, not the evidence. None of them watches the registry's publication stream for the consumer continuously, none reasons about the history of a package — who published the previous versions, how often, into which lines — and none keeps evidence of a publication once the registry purges it. The compromised-publisher class [12], the dominant class of 2026 [1], [4], falls between them. These three gaps define the proposed system.

In short, the new solution must be a continuous, metadata-first monitor of the npm registry with a cheap first stage, a budgeted second stage, explainable alerts, durable evidence and a reproducible evaluation. Chapter 2 turns these statements into requirements.

Chapter 2. System requirements

2.1 Vision and goals

Vision statement. For security engineers and maintainers who depend on the npm registry, the npm Publication Monitor is a continuous early-warning service that flags suspicious package publications minutes after they happen, before the code is analysed and before the registry removes the evidence. Unlike content scanners and vulnerability databases, which see malware only once it is recognised, the monitor works on publication metadata and package history, where a compromised publisher is visible and a valid attestation proves nothing.

Business goals. (G1) Reduce the window between a malicious publication and the first actionable signal from hours to minutes. (G2) Make the signal cheap enough to run over the whole registry on one node within the quotas of public APIs. (G3) Make every alert explainable and verifiable by an analyst. (G4) Keep evidence of every flagged publication after the registry purges it. (G5) Measure the residual risk after the registry's content gate with a reproducible method.

2.3 Project scope

In scope. Continuous collection of the registry change feed with a persisted cursor; stage 1 — temporal, nominal and version lanes and a watchlist — without network requests; stage 2 — enrichment from five sources under quotas; weighted-sum scoring, evidence snapshots, maintainer-set history, analyst verdicts and a filter by a consumer's dependency list; a REST API with an OpenAPI 3.1 contract [35] and a WebSocket stream; a single-page dashboard usable on a phone; containerised deployment with health checks, central logs and an external uptime check; a privacy notice, retention periods and an exclusion list (Section 2.8); replay-based evaluation on the recorded background.

Out of scope. Analysis of package content (the layer the registry's gate already covers); machine learning (no labelled corpus, explainability is a requirement); comparison of the tarball with the source repository (high traffic cost for a signal that cheaper features approximate); dependency confusion (needs a second data source — corporate namespaces); blocking or quarantining packages (the system informs, it does not act on the registry).

2.4 Solution concept

The system is a pipeline of six processes around one PostgreSQL database (Figure 2.1; component view in Figure B.5). The collector polls the registry change feed every five seconds and writes each page of events together with the new cursor in one transaction; every event records the interval in which it can have appeared, so catch-up after downtime does not look like a burst. Stage 1 scores every event from the journal alone: bursts per package, scope and name cluster against a 24-hour baseline, breadth of "wake-ups" of mature packages across unrelated namespaces, similarity of the name to the top-10 000 names, revision jumps and regressions, membership of the top-1 000 watchlist. An event becomes a candidate at a preliminary score of 2. Stage 2 enriches candidates under per-source quotas and compares the publication with the package's own history: publisher against previous releases, maintainers and the age of the e-mail domain, lifecycle scripts, size and file count, dependencies, release lines, repository state. An alert needs a final score of 4 with at least 2 points of stage-2 evidence; a version or package the registry has already removed is a confirmation, not evidence, because nothing is left to show. The web process serves the REST API, a WebSocket stream of events and results, Swagger UI and the dashboard. The popular process refreshes the top-10 000 names daily; migrate applies schema migrations.

2.6 Use case scenarios

The use case diagram with all actors is Figure B.3 in Appendix B.

UC1 — Review an alert. Actor: security analyst. Precondition: the pipeline is running; an event has reached the alert threshold; the analyst has the analyst token. Main flow: (1) the analyst opens the Alerts list and sees the new alert with its package, versions, score and feature chips; (2) the analyst opens the event card; (3) the card shows the score composition, the version timeline with the publisher of every version, the feature table with values and thresholds, the comparison with the previous version, the answers of each source and the evidence snapshot; (4) the analyst checks the package on the registry and the repository; (5) the analyst records the verdict confirmed with a note; (6) the system stores the verdict and shows it on the card and in the lists. Alternative flow A1 (false positive): at step 5 the analyst records false positive; the verdict is stored; later alerts for the same package remain visible but the analyst sees the earlier verdict on the package page. Alternative flow A2 (source skipped): at step 3 one source shows "skipped: quota"; the analyst decides from the remaining evidence or waits for the quota window; the skip is recorded on the event. Alternative flow A3 (no token): at step 5 the request is refused; the card asks for the token.

UC2 — Check an unexpected release of own package. Actor: package maintainer. Precondition: the maintainer has the package name. Main flow: (1) the maintainer types the name into the search box; (2) the package page lists every journal event of the package with chips, scores and verdicts, and the history of the maintainer set; (3) the maintainer opens the latest event; (4) the timeline shows that the last version was published by an account different from the previous releases, with the feature publisher_new; (5) the maintainer revokes the token on the registry and notifies users. Alternative flow A1 (no events): at step 2 the page states that the package has no events in the observation period. Alternative flow A2 (benign release): at step 4 the publisher matches the history and no stage-2 feature fired; the maintainer closes the page.

The system sequence diagram of UC1 (Figure B.4 in Appendix B) starts when the registry publishes a version: the collector stores the event, stage 1 scores it, stage 2 enriches it and stores the evidence, the web process pushes the result to the open dashboard over the WebSocket, and the analyst records the verdict.

2.8 Legal and ethical requirements

Personal data. The system processes only data that the registry publishes openly: package names, version numbers, timestamps, publisher user names and the e-mail addresses attached to versions and maintainers. User names and e-mail addresses are personal data under the GDPR [28]. The processing is not neutral: an alert attaches an automatic label to a named account, and before calibration most of about 250 alerts a day were false.

Legal basis and balancing test. Legitimate interest, Article 6(1)(f), assessed in three steps. Purpose: early detection of compromised publications protects the users of the ecosystem and the maintainers, whose accounts are the ones stolen. Necessity: the publisher of a version is the main signal of a compromised account (Section 1.3), and no less intrusive data carries it. Balancing: the data is public and published in a professional role, but a false label can harm a reputation; the measures below limit that risk.

Measures. Access: the dashboard and the API are open only to the analysts of the deployment; no public page shows alerts with account names, and a public demonstration shows them pseudonymised. Wording: an alert is a signal for review with its evidence, never a statement that an account is malicious; a false positive verdict is shown with every later alert of the package. Minimisation: an e-mail address is used only to derive the domain for the RDAP look-up and is never shown or sent to a third party. Retention: snapshots of candidates without an alert are deleted after 30 days; alerts, their snapshots and verdicts are deleted six months after the defence; the journal holds package names only. Transparency, Article 14: the data is not collected from the subjects and informing each of them would take disproportionate effort, so Article 14(5)(b) applies; a public privacy notice in the repository and on the dashboard states the purpose, the data, the legal basis, the retention and a contact. Right to object, Article 21: on request an account goes on an exclusion list; its name is removed from stored snapshots and is no longer stored, unless a confirmed incident gives compelling grounds (Article 21(1)). The deployment is not commercial.

Participants. No interviews or surveys were held, so no consent forms were needed. Analysts outside the project who record verdicts in a later phase will be informed that their verdicts are stored with a timestamp and used in the evaluation.

Licences. The project's code is published under the MIT licence; its runtime dependencies are permissive (Preact, ws, Node.js — MIT; htm, swagger-ui-dist — Apache-2.0; postgres.js — Unlicense; PostgreSQL — PostgreSQL licence). External data sources are used within their published terms and rate limits [5], [24]–[27], the registry data for non-commercial analysis.

Industry standards. The dashboard follows WCAG 2.1 level AA [29] where it applies to a data-dense tool: contrast ≥ 4.5:1 in both colour schemes, keyboard operability with a visible focus indicator, ARIA attributes for the live regions, status never by colour alone. Secure development follows the NIST SP 800-218 [9] practices that fit a one-person project: pinned dependencies, no secrets in the repository, reviewed changes, automated tests.
